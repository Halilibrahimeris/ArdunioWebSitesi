A breadboard is a plastic block full of holes that lets you build circuits
without soldering. There are metal clips under the holes, and **some holes are
connected to each other.**

The whole skill comes down to one question: **which holes are connected?**

<WiringDiagram file="breadboard-baglantilar.svg" caption="Holes outlined in the same colour are connected to each other" />

## Three rules

### 1. Vertical groups: columns of five

In the large middle area, **the five holes in one column** are connected.

So `a5`, `b5`, `c5`, `d5` and `e5` are all the same point. Put a wire in one and
a resistor in another, and you have connected them.

### 2. The centre channel splits everything in two

The groove down the middle is not decoration — it is **an electrical wall.**

`e5` and `f5` look like the same column but they are **not connected.** The
upper block (f-j) and the lower block (a-e) are completely separate.

The channel has a purpose: an integrated circuit sits astride it, so each of its
legs lands in its own column.

### 3. Horizontal power rails: connected end to end

The long strips along the edges (marked with a red **+** and a blue **−** line)
are **connected along their entire length.** A wire in hole 2 of the `−` rail is
at the same point as a wire in hole 28.

<Callout lang="en" variant="tip" title="Why do the power rails exist?">
Most circuits have many parts that all connect to the same GND. Rather than
running each one back to the board separately, you connect them all to the `−`
rail and then run **a single wire** from that rail to the board's GND pin.

That is how every project on this site handles ground.
</Callout>

## Hole addresses

Every diagram on this site names holes like this:

| Notation | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `a5` | Lower block, row `a`, column 5 |
| `f12` | Upper block, row `f`, column 12 |
| `−6` | Bottom minus rail, level with hole 6 |
| `+6` | Bottom plus rail, level with hole 6 |

Row letters are printed down both sides of the board; column numbers run along
the top and through the middle.

<Callout lang="en" variant="warning" title="Your board may look different">
On some breadboards the power rails are the other way round: `+` on the outside
on one, `−` on another. **Go by the colour of the line, not the position** — a
red line means plus, a blue line means minus.

On some cheap boards the power rails are split in the middle. If you suspect
that, check both ends with a multimeter's continuity mode.
</Callout>

## The three most common mistakes

### Putting both LED legs in the same column

`a5` and `b5` are the same point. Put both legs of an LED there and you have
shorted it out — it will never light.

**Instead:** the legs must go into **different columns**, such as `a5` and `a6`.

### Forgetting the centre channel

Assuming a wire in `e10` connects to a resistor in `f10`. It does not. The
channel sits between them.

**Instead:** keep both in the same block, or bridge them with a jumper wire.

### Never running GND back to the board

Connecting every part's negative side to the `−` rail and then forgetting to run
that rail to the board's **GND** pin. The circuit is not closed, and nothing works.

**Instead:** always run one wire from the `−` rail to the board's GND pin.

<Callout lang="en" variant="danger" title="If you use an external supply">
When you power something from a separate 5V adapter, its **negative must also
join the board's GND**. Without a common zero point between the two circuits,
the signals become meaningless.

Projects 7 and 10 both come back to this.
</Callout>

## Before you plug anything in

1. **Power the board down** (unplug the USB cable)
2. Follow the hole addresses in the diagram one by one
3. When you are done, **check against the diagram** — then apply power

Repeat those three steps in every project. Most mistakes are caught by half a
minute of checking before the power goes on.
