The classic Arduino UNO uses 5V logic. Years of circuit diagrams, starter kits
and Tinkercad habits are built on that assumption.

**The UNO Q uses 3.3V logic.** Both of its brains — the Qualcomm chip and the
STM32 — live in the 3.3V world. That is a natural consequence of it being a
more modern board, but wire a module up out of old habit and you will get an
expensive lesson.

<Callout lang="en" variant="danger" title="The one-sentence version">
If a module's **output** is 5V, you cannot connect that output straight to a
UNO Q pin. You have to put something in between that brings the voltage down.
</Callout>

## Which direction is dangerous?

Most of the confusion comes from this: the problem is not *powering* a module
from 5V, it is a module *sending* a 5V signal back to the board.

- **Board to module (output):** the board drives 3.3V. Most 5V modules happily
  read 3.3V as a logic "1". This usually just works.
- **Module to board (input):** the module drives 5V. The pin expects 3.3V at
  most. **This is the dangerous direction.**

The board does have a 5V pin, and using it to power modules is fine. The
danger is a 5V *signal* reaching an input pin.

## Where each module stands

Every module used across the ten projects on this site:

| Module | Status | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| LED, button, buzzer, potentiometer | Safe | Connect directly |
| LDR photoresistor | Safe | Build a divider with 10 kΩ; output never exceeds 3.3V |
| DHT11 | Safe | **Power it from 3.3V** and its data line is 3.3V too |
| HC-SR501 PIR | Safe | Runs on 5V but its output is already 3.3V TTL |
| SSD1306 OLED | Safe | Natively 3.3V |
| **HC-SR04** | **Dangerous** | A voltage divider on ECHO is **mandatory** |
| WS2812 LED strip | Caution | 3.3V data works on short strips; long ones need a shifter |
| Relay module | Caution | May not trigger at 3.3V — drive it with a transistor |
| SG90 servo | Caution | The 3.3V signal is fine, but power it externally |

<Callout lang="en" variant="warning" title="Watch out for I2C LCDs">
The PCF8574 backpack behind a classic 16x2 I2C LCD **pulls the I2C lines up to
5V** with its own resistors, putting 5V onto SDA and SCL. That is why these
projects use an SSD1306 OLED instead: same price, natively 3.3V, no extra
circuitry at all.
</Callout>

## The voltage divider: two resistors

This is the circuit you will need most often. Two resistors bring a 5V signal
down to a safe level.

<WiringDiagram file="voltage-divider.svg" caption="Voltage divider for the HC-SR04 ECHO pin" />

The idea is simple: wire two resistors in series between 5V and GND. The
voltage at the point between them is set by the ratio of the two.

```
Vout = Vin × R2 / (R1 + R2)
```

With our values:

```
5.0 V × 3300 / (2200 + 3300) = 3.0 V
```

3.0 V sits below the board's 3.3 V limit and is comfortably high enough to
read as a "1". The 0.3 V of headroom absorbs resistor tolerances.

<Callout lang="en" variant="tip" title="Why not aim for exactly 3.3V?">
A resistor's real value can drift up to 5% from what is printed on it. Aim for
exactly 3.3V and an unlucky pair could give you 3.4-3.5V. Targeting 3.0V
removes that risk entirely.
</Callout>

### Why 2.2k and 3.3k?

Other pairs hit a similar ratio (1k + 2k, for instance). We picked these for
two reasons:

1. **They are standard values.** Every resistor kit contains 2.2 kΩ and 3.3 kΩ.
2. **They are high enough.** 5.5 kΩ total draws only about 0.9 mA. Much smaller
   resistors waste current; much larger ones slow the signal's edges down.

## Verify it by measuring

If you have a multimeter, measure the divider output *before* connecting it to
the board:

1. Wire the HC-SR04 to 5V and GND, and build the divider
2. Put the multimeter between the divider's midpoint and GND
3. Do **not** connect it to the board yet

The HC-SR04 does not output continuously, so you will read a low average. To
be certain, feed the divider's input from the 5V pin directly and measure the
output — you should see between 2.9 and 3.1 V.

## Three rules that keep the board alive

1. **Power down before you wire.** Every single time.
2. **Never join 3V3 straight to GND.** That is a short circuit.
3. **Read the module's datasheet.** If "output voltage" or "TTL level" says 5V,
   put a divider in between.
