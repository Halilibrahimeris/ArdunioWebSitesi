  - q: None of the LEDs light up.
    a: >-
      Check which way round the LEDs are. The long leg (anode) goes to the
      resistor and the short leg (cathode) to the GND rail. A backwards LED
      simply will not light — it is not damaged, so just turn it around.
  - q: The LEDs look very dim.
    a: >-
      That is expected. The board supplies 3.3V rather than 5V, so LEDs look
      dimmer than on a classic Arduino UNO. If you want more brightness, swap
      the 220Ω for 150Ω — but do not go below 100Ω.
  - q: The pedestrian request triggers when I have not pressed anything.
    a: >-

  - q: Nothing appears on the matrix.
    a: >-
      The Arduino_LED_Matrix library ships with the UNO Q core; there is nothing
      to install. First confirm that matrix.begin() is called inside setup().
      Then check the timing: while the board boots, the matrix shows the Linux
      boot logo for 20-30 seconds — run the sketch after that has finished.
  - q: The light does not go red immediately when I press the button.
    a: >-
      That is by design. Like a real traffic light, green stays on for another
      1.5 seconds before switching to amber. An instant change would be

This first project introduces the board and covers the two most fundamental
things an Arduino does: switching a pin on and off, and reading a button. It
sounds simple — but we will hide an idea inside it that real embedded systems
use every day: **doing several things at once without ever waiting.**

<Callout lang="en" variant="info" title="Read the guides first">
If this is your first time with the board, work through the App Lab setup and
board tour guides before starting. This project assumes your setup is done.

**If you do not know which breadboard holes connect to which**, read
[How Does a Breadboard Work?](/en/guides/how-a-breadboard-works/) first. It
takes five minutes and makes every step below make sense.
</Callout>

## Connection table

Every connection in the diagram above, listed one by one. `a6` means **lower
block, row `a`, column 6**. Row letters are printed down both sides of the
board and column numbers along the top and bottom.

Three tips for reading the diagram: every wire has **its own colour** and a
small label at its end naming the pin it comes from; **no wire crosses
another** (the LED wires arrive through the centre channel and drop straight
into their column, the button wire loops around the top of the board); the
bottom **−** rail carries GND and the **+** rail carries 3.3V.

### Wires from the board to the breadboard

| On the board | On the breadboard | Wire colour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `D2` | `e3` | blue |
| `D3` | `e11` | purple |
| `D4` | `e19` | orange |
| `D7` | `j26` (upper block, the button's left column) | pink |
| `3V3` | `+1` (bottom plus rail) | red |
| `GND` | `-1` (bottom minus rail) | black |

### Parts on the breadboard

| Part | One end | Other end |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 220 Ω resistor | `c3` | `c6` |
| 220 Ω resistor | `c11` | `c14` |
| 220 Ω resistor | `c19` | `c22` |
| Red LED | long leg `a6` | short leg `a7` |
| Amber LED | long leg `a14` | short leg `a15` |
| Green LED | long leg `a22` | short leg `a23` |
| Jumper (cathode to GND) | `b7` | `-7` |
| Jumper (cathode to GND) | `b15` | `-15` |
| Jumper (cathode to GND) | `b23` | `-23` |
| Button | columns 26 and 28, **straddling the centre channel** | |
| 10 kΩ resistor | `b26` | `-26` |
| Jumper (+ rail to button) | `+28` | `a28` |

<Callout lang="en" variant="tip" title="Why these columns?">
The column numbers are arbitrary — you could shift the whole thing five columns
to the right. What matters is **which parts share a column.**

The red LED's long leg is in `a6` and the 220 Ω resistor's end is in `c6`.
Both are in column 6, so they are connected — no wire between them, but
electrically the same point.
</Callout>

<Step n={2} title="Disconnect power">

**Unplug** the USB-C cable before you wire anything. Plugging wires in while
the board is powered can cause permanent damage the moment you accidentally
bridge two pins.

<Callout lang="en" variant="warning">
This is the rule for every hardware project you will ever build:
**power off first, then wire.**
</Callout>

<Step n={3} title="Place the three LEDs on the breadboard">

All three LEDs follow the same pattern; only the column number shifts. Build
the red one first and the other two become easy.

**Tell the legs apart:**

- **Long leg** = anode (+), goes to the resistor
- **Short leg** = cathode (−), goes to GND

<Callout lang="en" variant="warning" title="The two legs go in different columns">
An LED's legs must go into **different columns**, such as `a6` and `a7`. Put
them in the same column (`a6` and `b6`) and you short the LED out — it will
never light.
</Callout>

**The red LED — four steps:**

1. **Resistor:** put one leg of a 220 Ω resistor in `c3` and the other in `c6`
2. **LED:** put the red LED's **long** leg in `a6` and its **short** leg in `a7`
3. **Signal wire:** run a blue wire from the board's **D2** pin to `e3`
4. **Ground wire:** run a black wire from `b7` to `-7` on the bottom minus rail

Why it works: `e3` and `c3` share a column, so the resistor's left end is now on
D2. `c6` and `a6` share a column, so its right end is on the LED's long leg.
`a7` and `b7` share a column, so the LED's short leg reaches the minus rail.

**The amber LED** is the same thing eight columns right: `c11`-`c14`, LED
`a14`/`a15`, purple wire **D3** → `e11`, jumper `b15` → `-15`.

**The green LED** is eight columns further: `c19`-`c22`, LED `a22`/`a23`,
orange wire **D4** → `e19`, jumper `b23` → `-23`.

Finally run a black wire from the board's **GND** pin to `-1` on the bottom
minus rail. All three LEDs reach ground through that rail.

<Callout lang="en" variant="danger" title="Never skip the resistor">
Wire an LED straight to a pin and it draws unlimited current, destroying both
the LED and the board's output stage. A 220Ω resistor holds the current at
roughly 6 mA at 3.3V — safe for the LED and for the pin.
</Callout>

</Step>

<Step n={4} title="Wire the button and its pull-down resistor">

The button wiring looks more complicated than it needs to be, but there is a
reason for it.

**Place the button.** A tactile button has four legs and sits **straddling the
centre channel**: two legs in the upper block (row `f`) and two in the lower
block (row `e`). Put its left legs in column 26 and its right legs in column
28 — near the right end of the board, well away from the LEDs.

<Callout lang="en" variant="tip" title="How is the button wired inside?">
The two legs on the same side (either side of the channel) are already
connected internally. **Pressing** the button joins the left side to the right.

So column 26 and column 28 connect only while the button is held down.
</Callout>

Now three more connections:

1. **Voltage:** run a red wire from the board's **3V3** pin to `+1` on the
   bottom plus rail, then fit a short red jumper from `+28` to `a28`. The plus
   rail is connected end to end, so 3.3V reaches the button's right column.
2. **Signal:** run a pink wire from the board's **D7** pin to `j26`. `j26` is in
   the upper block, in the same column as the button's left leg (`f26`) — the
   wire loops around the top of the board and never crosses the LED wires.
3. **Pull-down resistor:** put one leg of the 10 kΩ resistor in `b26` and the other in `-26`

That 10kΩ resistor is a *pull-down*. Its job is to hold D7 at zero (at GND)
while the button is not pressed. Leave it out and D7 connects to nothing at
all when you release the button — it floats. A floating pin acts like an
aerial, picking up electrical noise and reading random ones and zeros. Your
code is fine, yet the light keeps jumping into pedestrian mode on its own.

<Callout lang="en" variant="tip">
Pressed, the pin reads 3.3V (a 1); released, it reads GND through the resistor
(a 0). Because the resistor is 10kΩ, only 0.33 mA flows from 3V3 to GND while
the button is held — a negligible amount of wasted energy.
</Callout>

</Step>

<Step n={5} title="Check your wiring against the diagram">

Once everything is wired, tick off the **connection table** above line by line
before applying power. Then check these five things:

1. **LED orientation** — long leg in the left hole (`a6`, `a14`, `a22`)
2. **Each LED's legs are in different columns** — same column and it will not light
3. **The rails are not mixed up** — the GND wire sits on the **blue-lined minus
   (−) rail**, the 3V3 wire on the **red-lined plus (+) rail**
4. **No direct wire between 3V3 and GND**
5. **The pin numbers are right** — red D2, amber D3, green D4, button D7

<Callout lang="en" variant="danger">
Connecting 3V3 straight to GND is a short circuit. At best the board protects
itself and shuts down. Look twice before you plug in.
</Callout>

<Callout lang="en" variant="info" title="If you are trying it in Tinkercad">
Tinkercad has no Arduino UNO Q, only the classic **Arduino UNO**. Practising the
logic there is a good idea — the circuit is identical.

The one difference: on a classic UNO you wire the button to the **5V** pin,
because that board's logic is 5V. **On a real UNO Q wire it to 3V3.** The rule
on this site is never to feed 5V into any pin: D3 and the analog pins cannot
take it at all, and the others only tolerate it while the board is powered —
rather than memorise the exceptions, treat every pin as 3.3V.
</Callout>

<Step n={6} title="Write the code and upload it">

Open `sketch/sketch.ino` and paste in the contents from the **Code** section
below. Then plug the USB-C cable in and press **Run**.

The heart of the program is these three lines inside `loop()`:

```cpp
void loop() {
  readButton();      // Read the button on every pass
  updatePhase();     // Advance the phase once its time is up
  drawMatrix();      // Refresh the matrix
}
```

None of these three ever waits. Each is called thousands of times a second,
and each time it checks the clock and asks *"is it my turn yet?"* If not, it
returns immediately.

The classic `delay(6000)` approach could not do this: the board would freeze
for six seconds, and a button press during that time would go unnoticed.

<Callout lang="en" variant="tip" title="Remember this pattern">
Using `millis()` to ask "has enough time passed?" is one of the most common
patterns in embedded software. It comes back in every project after this one.
</Callout>

</Step>

<Step n={7} title="Run it and experiment">

The lights should start cycling. While you watch, try these:

1. **Look at the matrix.** The same light on your breadboard is drawn there
   too. The vertical bar on the right edge shows how much of the phase is left.
2. **Press the button while it is green.** A small dot starts blinking in the
   top-left — the pedestrian request is registered. Green switches to amber
   within 1.5 seconds.
3. **Press it while it is red.** Nothing happens, because it is already the
   pedestrian's turn to cross.

<Callout lang="en" variant="info" title="Try it yourself">
Change the numbers in the `PHASE_MS` array to make the phases shorter or
longer. Set `GREEN_CUTOFF_MS` to 0 and the button switches the light to amber
instantly — then think about why real crossings do not work that way.
</Callout>

</Step>
