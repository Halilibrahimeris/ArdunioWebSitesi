  Your first build: three LEDs cycling like a real traffic light, with a button
  that lets a pedestrian cross. Meanwhile the board's own 8x13 LED matrix draws
  a miniature copy of the light on your breadboard.

troubleshooting:
  - q: None of the LEDs light up.
    a: >-
      Check which way round the LEDs are. The long leg (anode) goes to the
      resistor and the short leg (cathode) to the GND rail. A backwards LED
      simply will not light — it is not damaged, so just turn it around.
  - q: The LEDs look very dim.
    a: >-
      That is expected. The board supplies 3.3V rather than 5V, so LEDs look
      dimmer than on a classic Arduino UNO. If you want more brightness, swap
      the 220Ω for 150Ω — but do not go below 100Ω.
  - q: The pedestrian request triggers when I have not pressed anything.
    a: >-
      Almost always a missing or disconnected pull-down resistor. Without it,
      pin D7 floats and picks up electrical noise from the air. Confirm that
      the 10kΩ resistor connects D7 to GND.
  - q: Nothing appears on the matrix.
    a: >-
      Make sure the Arduino_LED_Matrix library is installed in App Lab. If not,
      add it from the Libraries section and recompile the sketch. Also confirm
      that matrix.begin() is called inside setup().
  - q: The light does not go red immediately when I press the button.
    a: >-
      That is by design. Like a real traffic light, green stays on for another
      1.5 seconds before switching to amber. An instant change would be
      dangerous.

This first project introduces the board and covers the two most fundamental
things an Arduino does: switching a pin on and off, and reading a button. It
sounds simple — but we will hide an idea inside it that real embedded systems
use every day: **doing several things at once without ever waiting.**

<Callout lang="en" variant="info" title="Read the guides first">
If this is your first time with the board, work through the App Lab setup and
board tour guides before starting. This project assumes your setup is done.
</Callout>

<Step n={1} title="Create a new app in App Lab">

Open App Lab, choose **Create → New App** and name it `traffic-light`.

App Lab prepares a three-part folder for you. This project only uses the
`sketch/` folder, because everything happens on the microcontroller (the
STM32) — we do not need the Linux side at all.

<PhotoSlot lang="en" caption="Creating a new app in App Lab" />

</Step>

<Step n={2} title="Disconnect power">

**Unplug** the USB-C cable before you wire anything. Plugging wires in while
the board is powered can cause permanent damage the moment you accidentally
bridge two pins.

<Callout lang="en" variant="warning">
This is the rule for every hardware project you will ever build:
**power off first, then wire.**
</Callout>

</Step>

<Step n={3} title="Place the three LEDs on the breadboard">

Push the LEDs into the breadboard leaving a spare row between each one. The
two legs of each LED must sit in **different** rows — put them in the same row
and you short the LED out, so it never lights.

Telling the legs apart is easy:

- **Long leg** = anode (+), goes to the resistor
- **Short leg** = cathode (−), goes to the GND rail

Now connect a 220Ω resistor to each LED's long leg and run the other end of
the resistor to these pins:

| LED | Pin |
| --- | --- |
| Red | D2 |
| Amber | D3 |
| Green | D4 |

Connect all three short legs to the breadboard's negative (−) rail, and that
rail to the board's **GND** pin.

<Callout lang="en" variant="danger" title="Never skip the resistor">
Wire an LED straight to a pin and it draws unlimited current, destroying both
the LED and the board's output stage. A 220Ω resistor holds the current at
roughly 6 mA at 3.3V — safe for the LED and for the pin.
</Callout>

</Step>

<Step n={4} title="Wire the button and its pull-down resistor">

The button wiring looks more complicated than it needs to be, but there is a
reason for it.

Connect one leg of the button to the **3V3** pin. Connect the opposite leg to
both the **D7** pin and, through a **10kΩ** resistor, to **GND**.

That 10kΩ resistor is a *pull-down*. Its job is to hold D7 at zero (at GND)
while the button is not pressed. Leave it out and D7 connects to nothing at
all when you release the button — it floats. A floating pin acts like an
aerial, picking up electrical noise and reading random ones and zeros. Your
code is fine, yet the light keeps jumping into pedestrian mode on its own.

<Callout lang="en" variant="tip">
Pressed, the pin reads 3.3V (a 1); released, it reads GND through the resistor
(a 0). Because the resistor is 10kΩ, only 0.33 mA flows from 3V3 to GND while
the button is held — a negligible amount of wasted energy.
</Callout>

</Step>

<Step n={5} title="Check your wiring against the diagram">

Once everything is wired, check it again before applying power. Look
especially for these two:

- No LED is in backwards (long leg goes to the resistor)
- There is **no** direct wire between 3V3 and GND

<Callout lang="en" variant="danger">
Connecting 3V3 straight to GND is a short circuit. At best the board protects
itself and shuts down. Look twice before you plug in.
</Callout>

</Step>

<Step n={6} title="Write the code and upload it">

Open `sketch/sketch.ino` and paste in the contents from the **Code** section
below. Then plug the USB-C cable in and press **Run**.

The heart of the program is these three lines inside `loop()`:

```cpp
void loop() {
  readButton();      // Read the button on every pass
  updatePhase();     // Advance the phase once its time is up
  drawMatrix();      // Refresh the matrix
}
```

None of these three ever waits. Each is called thousands of times a second,
and each time it checks the clock and asks *"is it my turn yet?"* If not, it
returns immediately.

The classic `delay(6000)` approach could not do this: the board would freeze
for six seconds, and a button press during that time would go unnoticed.

<Callout lang="en" variant="tip" title="Remember this pattern">
Using `millis()` to ask "has enough time passed?" is one of the most common
patterns in embedded software. It comes back in every project after this one.
</Callout>

</Step>

<Step n={7} title="Run it and experiment">

The lights should start cycling. While you watch, try these:

1. **Look at the matrix.** The same light on your breadboard is drawn there
   too. The vertical bar on the right edge shows how much of the phase is left.
2. **Press the button while it is green.** A small dot starts blinking in the
   top-left — the pedestrian request is registered. Green switches to amber
   within 1.5 seconds.
3. **Press it while it is red.** Nothing happens, because it is already the
   pedestrian's turn to cross.

<Callout lang="en" variant="info" title="Try it yourself">
Change the numbers in the `PHASE_MS` array to make the phases shorter or
longer. Set `GREEN_CUTOFF_MS` to 0 and the button switches the light to amber
instantly — then think about why real crossings do not work that way.
</Callout>

</Step>
