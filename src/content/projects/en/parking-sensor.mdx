This project has two halves. The first is the fun one: a circuit that behaves
like a car's parking sensor. The second is the most important technical lesson
on this site.

<Callout lang="en" variant="voltage" linkToGuide>
The HC-SR04's **ECHO pin outputs 5V**. The UNO Q's pins are designed for 3.3V
logic; wiring ECHO directly puts the pin at risk (on D3 or an analog pin it is
certain damage). In this project we put a two-resistor circuit in between — and
you will understand exactly why it works.
</Callout>

## How does an ultrasonic sensor work?

An HC-SR04 behaves rather like a bat: it emits a sound too high for humans to
hear (40 kHz) and waits for the echo to come back.

Since we know how fast sound travels through air — roughly 343 metres per
second — the time the echo takes tells us the distance.

The sensor has four pins:

| Pin | Purpose | Direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| VCC | Power, **5V** | — |
| TRIG | "Measure now" | Board → sensor |
| ECHO | Duration of the echo | **Sensor → board (5V!)** |
| GND | Ground | — |

Only **ECHO** is dangerous. The board sends 3.3V to TRIG, which the sensor
happily accepts as a "1".

<Step n={1} title="Power down and place the sensor">

Unplug the USB-C cable. Push the HC-SR04 into one edge of the breadboard with
its two "eyes" facing open space, leaving at least 20-30 cm clear in front.

</Step>

<Step n={2} title="Wire power and TRIG">

This is the safe side:

- Sensor **VCC** → board **5V**
- Sensor **GND** → board **GND**
- Sensor **TRIG** → board **D10**

<Callout lang="en" variant="tip" title="Why VCC on 5V?">
An HC-SR04 sometimes runs at 3.3V, but its range shortens and readings get
unreliable. Powering the sensor from 5V is not the problem — feeding its 5V
*output* into the board is.
</Callout>

</Step>

<Step n={3} title="Build the voltage divider — do not skip this">

Now the critical part. **Do not connect ECHO to D9 yet.**

Pick an empty row on the breadboard, then:

1. Connect one end of the **2.2kΩ** resistor to the sensor's **ECHO** pin
2. Bring its other end to the empty row — this is the **midpoint**
3. Connect one end of the **3.3kΩ** resistor to that midpoint and the other to **GND**
4. Run a wire from the midpoint to the board's **D9** pin

<WiringDiagram file="voltage-divider.svg" caption="How the voltage divider works" />

The arithmetic:

```
Vout = 5.0 V × 3300 / (2200 + 3300) = 3.0 V
```

Even when the sensor drives 5V, the voltage reaching D9 is 3.0V — safe for the
board and comfortably high enough to read as a "1".

<Callout lang="en" variant="danger" title="The resistor order matters">
2.2kΩ goes **on top** (the ECHO side) and 3.3kΩ **underneath** (the GND side).
Swapped, the output becomes 5.0 × 2200/5500 = 2.0V. The board still reads it,
but with less margin against noise.
</Callout>

</Step>

<Step n={4} title="Verify with a multimeter, if you have one">

If you own a multimeter, do not skip this — measuring before you connect beats
regretting it afterwards.

1. Do **not** attach the wire to D9 yet
2. Temporarily connect the input of the 2.2kΩ resistor to the **5V** pin
3. Put the multimeter between the midpoint and GND
4. You should read between **2.9 and 3.1 V**

If the reading is right, remove the temporary wire, reconnect ECHO and attach
the D9 wire.

</Step>

<Step n={5} title="Wire the buzzer and LEDs">

Familiar from project 2:

- Passive buzzer + 220 Ω in series → **D8** and **GND**
- Red LED + 220Ω → **D2**
- Amber LED + 220Ω → **D3**
- Green LED + 220Ω → **D4**

</Step>

<Step n={6} title="Upload the code">

Paste the code in and press **Run**. Open App Lab's serial monitor.

Move your hand in front of the sensor and watch the distances stream past.

This is the line that turns time into distance:

```cpp
distanceCm = (echoUs * 0.0343) / 2.0;
```

`0.0343` is the speed of sound in cm per microsecond. We halve it because the
pulse travels out **and back** — the time we measured covers twice the distance.

</Step>

<Step n={7} title="Try the zones">

Move your hand in slowly and watch the three zones:

| Distance | LED | Sound |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Over 100 cm | Green | Silent |
| 50-100 cm | Green | Occasional beep |
| 30-50 cm | Amber | Quickening beeps |
| Under 30 cm | Red | Continuous |

Notice how the beep interval is worked out: `mapFloat()` maps the distance
straight onto a waiting time, so the transitions are smooth rather than sudden.

<Callout lang="en" variant="info" title="Why our own mapFloat?">
Arduino's built-in `map()` only handles integers. Our distance is a `float`, so
using `map()` would throw away precision. A five-line version of our own fixes
that.
</Callout>

</Step>
