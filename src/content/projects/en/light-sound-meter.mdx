In the first project the pins knew only two states: on or off. The real world
does not work that way — a room is not "bright" or "dark" but somewhere in
between.

This project uses the board's **analog inputs**. These pins measure a voltage
and hand you back a number.

<Callout lang="en" variant="info" title="What does the 3.3V reference mean?">
`analogRead()` converts the voltage on a pin into a number from 0 to 1023.
That 1023 corresponds to the board's **3.3V**, not the 5V of a classic UNO. So
a reading of 512 means roughly 1.65V is present on the pin.
</Callout>

<Step n={1} title="Power down before building">

As with every project: unplug the USB-C cable. This build adds five LEDs, a
buzzer and two analog sensors, so the breadboard gets busier.

</Step>

<Step n={2} title="Wire the LDR as a voltage divider">

An LDR (photoresistor) is a component whose **resistance changes** with light.
Bright light lowers it; darkness raises it.

But the board cannot measure resistance — only voltage. So we pair the LDR
with a fixed resistor to turn that changing resistance into a changing voltage:

- **3V3** → LDR → **A0**
- **A0** → 10kΩ resistor → **GND**

In bright light the LDR's resistance drops, the voltage at A0 rises, and
`analogRead()` returns a large number. In the dark, the opposite happens.

<Callout lang="en" variant="tip" title="This is exactly a voltage divider">
It is the same circuit the 3.3V guide describes for the HC-SR04. The
difference: there both resistors were fixed and the goal was to lower a
voltage. Here one of them is the sensor itself and the goal is to measure how
it changes.
</Callout>

</Step>

<Step n={3} title="Wire the potentiometer">

A potentiometer has three legs, and the order matters:

| Leg | Goes to |
| --- | --- |
| Left end | 3V3 |
| Middle (wiper) | A1 |
| Right end | GND |

As you turn the knob, the voltage on the middle leg sweeps between 0V and 3.3V.

<Callout lang="en" variant="warning">
Do **not** wire the outer legs to the 5V pin. If you do, the wiper can feed up
to 5V into A1 and damage it. Use 3V3.
</Callout>

</Step>

<Step n={4} title="Wire the LED bar and the buzzer">

Line up five LEDs side by side. Run each long leg through its own 220Ω
resistor to these pins: **D2, D3, D4, D5, D6**. The short legs go to the
breadboard's negative rail.

Do not wire the passive buzzer straight to **D8**; put a **220 Ω resistor** in
series: **D8** → 220 Ω → buzzer → **GND**. An electromagnetic buzzer's coil is
only ~16 Ω; without the resistor the pin would source far more than its 20 mA limit.

<Callout lang="en" variant="danger" title="It must be a passive buzzer">
An active buzzer contains its own oscillator, so applying voltage produces one
fixed tone and you cannot change its frequency. `tone()` only works with a
**passive** buzzer.
</Callout>

<Step n={5} title="Upload and open the serial monitor">

Paste the code in and press **Run**. Then open App Lab's **serial monitor**.

Lines like this should scroll past twice a second:

```
Light: 742   Threshold: 400   LEDs: 3
```

Cover the LDR with your hand: "Light" drops quickly and fewer LEDs stay lit.
Turn the potentiometer and "Threshold" moves.

<Step n={6} title="See what the smoothing does">

This line is the most instructive part of the project:

```cpp
smoothedLight = smoothedLight * (1.0 - SMOOTHING) + rawLight * SMOOTHING;
```

Rather than using the new reading directly, we blend it with the old one. With
`SMOOTHING` at 0.2 the new value carries only 20% of the weight, so sudden
jumps get flattened out.

Try it yourself:

1. Set `SMOOTHING` to **1.0**. Now there is no smoothing at all; the LEDs and
   the tone jitter, because raw ADC readings always wobble by a few counts.
2. Set it to **0.02**. Very smooth, but sluggish — the bar fills slowly after
   you take your hand away.
3. Go back to **0.2**. A reasonable balance for most sensors.

<Callout lang="en" variant="tip" title="This is a filter">
What you just wrote is called an *exponential moving average*, or a
*first-order low-pass filter*. In professional embedded software it is used in
exactly this three-line form.
</Callout>

</Step>

<Step n={7} title="Take it further">

You have a working circuit — now play with it:

- Change `TONE_MIN` and `TONE_MAX` to widen the range of pitches
